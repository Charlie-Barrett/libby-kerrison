The florist's almanac

What's in season, and what to do with it.

The British cutting year, flower by flower: when each is at its best, the colours and character it brings, and how we combine them in weddings and workshops. This page grows with the seasons — check back, it changes.

Spring — the impatient season

Everything arrives in a rush and nothing lasts long — which is exactly the charm. Design loose, let stems bend, and change the water often.

TulipMarch–April

Every colour but true blue; keeps growing in the vase, so arrangements move overnight. Use that — don't fight it.

Pairs with narcissi and flowering branches. A single-variety mass in a low bowl is spring's easiest trick.

NarcissiFebruary–April

From bold trumpet yellows to tiny scented paperwhites and creams.

Condition separately (their sap shortens other flowers' lives), then combine with tulips and blossom.

RanunculusApril–May

Layer upon layer of petals; the closest spring gets to a rose. Soft pastels through to burgundy.

The wedding workhorse of late spring — bouquets, buttonholes, everything.

Blossom & branchesMarch–May

Cherry, apple, hawthorn. Height, structure and a week of falling petals.

Large-scale ceremony work; three branches beat thirty stems.

Summer — the meadow months

The British year at full volume. If you're marrying in June, July or August, almost everything below can be cut for you within days of the wedding.

CornflowerJune–July

Famous in blue, but the white and chocolate varieties are the designer's secret — June's most quietly elegant stems.

Thread through white-and-neutral palettes for depth without colour shouting; lovely in buttonholes.

AchilleaJune–September

Flat plates of tiny flowers in ochre, terracotta, soft pink and white. In a cool year it holds back early — not yet in full force — but earns its place in any theme it touches.

The great connector: it bridges colours that otherwise argue, and dries beautifully for autumn work.

Sweet peaJune–August

The scent of a British summer; ruffled pastels and rich purples.

Short-stemmed and delicate — cluster low in bouquets and jam jars where noses can reach them.

CosmosJuly–October

Weightless single flowers on wiry stems, white through candy pink.

The "just gathered" look: let them float an inch above the rest of the arrangement.

DahliaAugust–October

Arrives late summer and takes over — pompoms to dinner-plates, café-au-lait to near-black.

Focal flowers: one to three per arrangement, everything else in service of them.

Autumn — texture & harvest

Colour deepens and texture takes the lead: seed heads, grasses, hips and the last great flush of dahlias before the frosts.

Dahlia (peak)September–October

At their absolute best now — the most generous wedding flower of the season.

Pair with grasses and copper foliage; a September wedding can be nearly all-dahlia.

Grasses & seed headsSeptember–November

Panicum, miscanthus, poppy and nigella pods, honesty.

Movement and light-catching haze around focal flowers; dry them and they last all winter.

Rosehips & crab appleOctober–November

Gloss, shine and berry-red punctuation.

Harvest tables, autumn buttonholes, and the first wreath bases of the year.

Chrysanthemum (heritage)October–November

Forget the garage forecourt — British heritage blooms are spidery, bronze and extraordinary.

The bridge flower that carries weddings to the very edge of winter.

Winter — the honest season

We won't pretend Britain grows roses in January. Winter is branches, evergreens, forced bulbs and the first brave flowers — sculptural, scented and genuinely seasonal.

HelleboreJanuary–March

Nodding cups in white, plum and speckled green — winter's quiet aristocrat.

Sear stem ends; use in low arrangements and winter bridal work.

Evergreens & foliageDecember–February

Pine, fir, eucalyptus, holly, ivy — the backbone of wreath season.

Our December wreath workshops build on moss bases: compostable, no plastic frames.

Forced bulbsDecember–February

Paperwhites, hyacinths, amaryllis grown into flower indoors.

Scent and life in the darkest weeks; pots and bulb gardens over cut stems.

Branches & catkinsFebruary

Willow, hazel, early blossom forced into flower in the warm.

Sculptural minimalism for winter weddings — height without a single imported bloom.
